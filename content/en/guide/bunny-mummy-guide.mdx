## What Is a Bunny Mummy? The Doe Behind the Nest

A bunny mummy does more in eight weeks than most pet owners expect in a year. She carries a litter, builds a nest from her own fur, kindles in the dark, nurses on a schedule nobody sees, and weans her kits — all while hiding most of the work from you. This bunny mummy guide walks through every stage so you can support her instead of accidentally interfering.

If your doe is pregnant, or you just discovered a squirming pile of pink kits in the hutch, the first thing to understand is vocabulary. The mother rabbit is a **doe**. The babies are **kits**. The birth is **kindling**. Once those three words click, every rabbit-care article you read suddenly makes sense.

Rabbits are unusual among small pets because the doe does almost everything alone. She feeds her young for only a few minutes a day, leaves the nest for long stretches, and can breed again within hours of giving birth. Understanding that biology is the difference between a calm, healthy litter and a preventable tragedy.

### Quick-Reference Facts for a Bunny Mummy

| Stage | Typical timing | What happens | Your job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gestation | 28–32 days (usually around 31) | Kits develop in both uterine horns | Set up the nest box by day 28 |
| Nesting instinct | Final 1–3 days | Doe pulls fur, carries hay in her mouth | Provide soft bedding, stay calm |
| Kindling | Often at night or early morning | Doe delivers, cleans kits, eats afterbirth | Watch quietly, do not interrupt |
| Nursing | Once or twice daily, usually dawn and dusk | Each session lasts only a few minutes | Leave the litter alone |
| Eyes open | Around 10–14 days | Kits begin exploring the box | Refresh bedding, keep it dry |
| Weaning | 6–8 weeks | Kits shift to hay and pellets | Separate males by 8–10 weeks |

A healthy doe needs very little from you during most of this. The most common mistake new owners make is over-handling — checking the nest every hour, moving kits, or trying to "help" a mother who is already doing fine.

## Preparing the Nest Box: Timing, Materials, and Placement

The nest box is the single most important piece of equipment in this bunny mummy guide. Introduce it around day 26 to 28 of pregnancy, before the doe starts pulling fur. Early placement gives her time to rearrange it, and rearranging is how she claims it as hers.

Size the box to the doe rather than to a breed chart — roughly one and a half times her stretched-out length, with a low front lip so kits can eventually climb out but not tumble out in the first week. Wood and heavy plastic both work; wood breathes better, plastic cleans easier.

| Item | What to use | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Nest box | Wood or heavy plastic, low-sided front | Contains kits and holds warmth |
| Base bedding | Soft grass hay plus shredded paper | Absorbent, safe if nibbled |
| Top layer | The doe's own pulled fur | The best insulation available |
| Placement | Corner of the hutch, away from the door | Feels secure, reduces scattering |
| Timing | Day 26–28 of gestation | Lets the doe settle before kindling |
| Extras | Nothing scented, no cedar shavings | Strong odors stress does |

### Signs Kindling Is Close

- Fur pulling from the belly and dewlap
- Carrying mouthfuls of hay and stacking them in a corner
- Restlessness, digging, or refusing food the day before
- Visible, enlarged mammary glands
- A sudden shift from curious to secretive behavior

If you see fur in the box, kindling is usually within 24 to 48 hours. Resist the urge to hover. Breeders and rescue volunteers report that does kindle most smoothly in a quiet, dim hutch with no audience.

## The First 72 Hours After Kindling

Kindling itself is fast — often under 30 minutes for the whole litter. The doe licks each kit clean, eats the placenta, and settles the litter into the nest. If you happen to witness it and everything is progressing, stay back. Interruption is the main cause of scattered litters.

| Time window | What you may see | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 hours | Doe kindles, cleans kits, eats afterbirth | Observe from a distance only |
| 2–12 hours | Doe is away from the nest | Totally normal — does avoid the box |
| 12–24 hours | First nursing, usually at dawn or dusk | Confirm kits have round bellies |
| 24–48 hours | Doe may move kits if she feels unsafe | Cut noise, limit foot traffic |
| 48–72 hours | Kits look less wrinkled, fuzz appears | Quick daily headcount, warm hands |

Here is the myth worth killing: **a doe will not reject her kits because you touched them.** Domestic rabbits are not wild deer. Brief, gentle handling with clean, warm hands is fine and often necessary — especially if a kit gets dragged out of the nest.

If kits end up scattered on the wire, warm them in your hands or against your skin first, then return them to the nest together. A cold kit that is placed back immediately may be abandoned simply because it feels wrong to the doe.

## Feeding a Nursing Bunny Mummy

A lactating doe is working harder than at any other point in her life. Milk production is calorically expensive, and her water intake can rise dramatically. Free-feed her and keep a heavy ceramic bowl filled at all times — bottles work, but a bowl is easier to monitor and easier for a thirsty doe to drain.

| Nutrient | Why it matters | Good sources |
|---|---|---|
| Protein (roughly 16–18%) | Fuels milk production | Quality pellets, alfalfa for nursing does |
| Calcium | Milk and kit bone growth | Alfalfa hay, leafy greens |
| Fresh water | Milk is largely water | Bowl checked twice daily |
| Long-stem fiber | Prevents GI stasis in the doe | Unlimited grass hay |
| Moist greens | Hydration and vitamins | Romaine, parsley, cilantro |

### Feeding Rules That Prevent Problems

- Never make sudden diet changes while she is pregnant or nursing.
- Feed unlimited pellets during lactation, then taper after weaning.
- Keep alfalfa available for the nursing doe; switch kits to grass hay later.
- Wash all greens and never feed wilted or spoiled produce.
- Remove uneaten fresh food after a few hours to avoid spoilage.

A doe who stops eating for 12 hours or more is a medical emergency, not a mood. GI stasis can kill a rabbit within a day, and a nursing mother has no reserves to spare.

## Health Checks for the Bunny Mummy and Her Litter

Most litters need nothing beyond warmth, quiet, and food. The skill worth building is knowing which warning signs mean "wait and watch" and which mean "call the vet now."

| Warning sign | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Doe not eating 12+ hours | GI stasis, stress, pain | Emergency vet visit |
| Hot, hard, red mammary glands | Mastitis | Urgent vet care |
| Kits cold, squeaking, scattered | Nest disturbance or poor milk supply | Warm and return kits, monitor closely |
| Sunken, wrinkled kit bellies | Kits are not nursing | Check the doe, consult a vet |
| Heavy bleeding after kindling | Retained kit or tissue | Emergency vet care |
| No kits by day 32 | Delayed kindling or false pregnancy | Vet check |

For routine care questions, the [House Rabbit Society's rabbit health and care library](https://rabbit.org/) is one of the most reliable, vet-reviewed resources available to pet owners. Bookmark it before you need it.

One long-term note: if you are not deliberately breeding, spaying your doe removes the risk of pregnancy complications entirely and sharply reduces the risk of uterine cancer, which is common in unspayed female rabbits as they age. Talk to a rabbit-savvy veterinarian about timing.

## Weaning Timeline and What Comes Next

Weaning is gradual in rabbits. Kits start sampling solid food while still nursing, and the doe slowly reduces milk. Pushing this process faster than the kits are ready causes digestive upset, which is the leading killer of young rabbits.

| Age | Diet | Care notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 weeks | Doe's milk only | Minimal handling, dry nest |
| 2–3 weeks | First nibbles of hay and pellets | Clean the box, keep bedding dry |
| 3–4 weeks | Milk plus solids | Eyes open, kits start exploring |
| 4–6 weeks | Mostly solids | Watch for soft droppings |
| 6–8 weeks | Fully weaned | Separate from the doe |
| 8–10 weeks | Juvenile pellets and grass hay | Separate males from females |

### After Weaning: A Short Checklist

- Move kits to a clean, draft-free enclosure with fresh hay and water.
- Feed a gradual transition diet — never a sudden pellet switch.
- Weigh kits weekly; steady gain is the best health signal you have.
- Handle them daily so they stay social and easy to manage.
- Plan for rehoming no earlier than 8 weeks, ideally 10.
- Have a rabbit-savvy vet lined up before anything goes wrong.

The bunny mummy's job is done at weaning, and she usually recovers quickly once kits stop nursing. Give her a quiet week, unlimited hay, and a break from handling before you consider breeding again — or better, before you book that spay appointment.

## FAQ

**How long is a bunny mummy pregnant?**
Gestation runs 28 to 32 days, most commonly around 31. If your doe passes day 32 with no kits, have a veterinarian examine her — delayed kindling can mean a stuck kit or a false pregnancy.

**Will a bunny mummy reject her kits if I touch them?**
No. That is one of the most persistent myths in rabbit keeping. Domestic does generally tolerate brief handling. Keep it short, use warm hands, return each kit to the nest, and avoid heavy handling in the first few days.

**How often does a bunny mummy feed her kits?**
Usually once or twice a day, most often at dawn and dusk, for only a few minutes. If the kits' bellies look round and they are quiet and warm, they are being fed — even if you never see it happen.

**When can kits leave their mother?**
Kits are fully weaned between 6 and 8 weeks and should stay with the doe until at least 8 weeks. Separating earlier increases the risk of digestive problems and stress-related illness.
